Crypto Due Diligence Framework

Joe Nasr · Joenasriani · checklist reference, not an automated scanner

A structured way to decide what needs verification before trusting a cryptocurrency project, exchange, token, or DeFi protocol. The framework organizes evidence questions; it does not determine that an entity is legitimate or fraudulent.

01 / Identity

What entity is actually responsible?
  • Identify the legal entity, jurisdiction, directors, controlling persons, and public business records where available.
  • Verify regulatory claims directly in the relevant regulator's official register rather than relying on logos or marketing text.
  • Compare team biographies, employment claims, addresses, and company history against independent records.
What would count as a problem?

Contradictory identities, unverifiable licenses, false affiliations, or unexplained changes are reasons for further investigation. None of those signals alone proves fraud.

02 / Code

What can the software actually do?
  • Locate verified contract addresses and compare them with the addresses published by the project.
  • Inspect privileged functions such as minting, pausing, upgrades, blacklisting, ownership transfer, fee changes, and withdrawal controls.
  • Read published audit reports themselves. Confirm scope, version, date, unresolved findings, and whether deployed code matches the audited code.

Automated scanners can identify patterns worth inspection, but a scanner result should not be treated as a complete smart-contract audit.

03 / Assets

Who controls the money and supply?
  • Inspect token distribution, vesting, treasury control, liquidity ownership, multisig configuration, and concentration among large holders.
  • For custodial services, distinguish a public proof-of-reserves claim from evidence about liabilities, ownership, and controls.
  • Verify lock periods and liquidity claims on-chain rather than relying on screenshots.

04 / Chain

Does transaction behavior match the public story?
  • Trace large transfers, related wallets, funding origins, repeated circular flows, contract interactions, and sudden changes in holder concentration.
  • Separate observations from attribution. Two wallets interacting does not by itself prove common ownership or malicious coordination.
  • Record transaction hashes, block numbers, timestamps, and the chain used so findings remain reproducible.

05 / Infrastructure

Does the technical footprint support the claimed history?
  • Compare domain registration history, archived pages, public repositories, release history, package metadata, and published documentation.
  • Look for contradictions between launch dates, claimed development history, repositories, contracts, and archived marketing material.
  • Do not treat use of a CDN, privacy service, template, or young domain as proof of wrongdoing. They are context, not verdicts.

06 / Claims

Which promotional statements can be independently checked?
  • Verify partnerships through the claimed partner's own channels.
  • Check quotations, customer counts, rankings, return claims, audits, certifications, and institutional affiliations against primary sources.
  • Treat follower counts, engagement spikes, testimonials, and influencer promotion as signals requiring context rather than evidence of legitimacy.

07 / Record

Can another reviewer reproduce the finding?
  1. Record the exact claim being tested.
  2. Save the source URL, date, identifier, contract address, or transaction hash.
  3. Separate observation, inference, and allegation.
  4. Record alternative explanations.
  5. State what remains unknown.
Evidence boundary This page is a static due-diligence reference. It does not run blockchain surveillance, wallet clustering, fraud detection, smart-contract auditing, live alerts, identity verification, or incident response. A red flag is not proof of fraud, and an apparently clean review is not proof of safety. Financial, legal, regulatory, and security decisions require current primary evidence and appropriate qualified review.
