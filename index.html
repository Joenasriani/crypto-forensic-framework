Crypto Due Diligence Framework

Joe Nasr · evidence method · updated 17 September 2026

A reproducible method for testing claims about cryptocurrency projects, exchanges, tokens and DeFi protocols using public evidence—without converting warning signals into unsupported accusations.

01 / Question

What is this research trying to solve?

How can public evidence from legal-entity records, deployed code, smart contracts, on-chain activity, infrastructure, market data and public claims be organized into a defensible due-diligence process?

Core rule: a red flag is not proof of fraud, and the absence of a red flag is not proof of legitimacy.

02 / Method

  1. Define the target. Exact entity, token, contract, exchange, domain, wallet or claim.
  2. Fix jurisdiction and date. Regulatory conclusions are jurisdiction- and time-specific.
  3. State the claim under test. Begin with a proposition, not a verdict.
  4. Collect primary evidence first. Chain records, deployed code, official registries, filings, court/enforcement records and first-party technical material.
  5. Record provenance. URL, identifier, contract, transaction hash, block, timestamp, archive date, version or commit.
  6. Corroborate independently. Source agreement is not the same as source independence.
  7. Separate observation from inference. Interaction is not ownership; concentration is not manipulation; anonymity is not criminality.
  8. Test alternatives. Preserve plausible benign explanations and contradictory evidence.
  9. Bound confidence. Confidence applies to the specific claim, not the whole project.
  10. State falsifiers and unknowns. Record what would weaken, overturn or leave the conclusion unresolved.

03 / Sources

Tier 1 — Primary records

Blockchain transactions and contract state; verified deployed code; official corporate, regulator, sanctions, court and enforcement records; project-controlled repositories, governance records and first-party disclosures.

Tier 2 — Direct technical evidence

Reproducible contract analysis; audit reports whose scope/version match deployed code; archived sites; release histories; DNS, certificate, package and repository metadata obtained lawfully from public sources.

Tier 3 — Independent analysis

Peer-reviewed research, established blockchain/market analytics and reputable investigative reporting with transparent sourcing. Third-party wallet labels, clustering outputs and risk scores remain analytical claims until independently supported.

Tier 4 — Promotional and social signals

Marketing, testimonials, influencers, follower counts and engagement patterns can generate questions but should not independently establish legitimacy, fraud, ownership or coordination.

04 / Dimensions

Entity identity and regulatory claims

Legal entity, jurisdiction, controlling persons, registrations, licenses and affiliations. Verify against the relevant official register for the jurisdiction and date.

Code and smart contracts

Contract identity, upgradeability, privileged functions, minting, pausing, blacklisting, fees, custody and withdrawal controls. An automated scanner is triage—not a complete audit.

Assets and control

Supply, vesting, treasury, liquidity, multisig configuration, holder concentration, custody and liabilities. A proof-of-reserves claim does not by itself establish liabilities or solvency.

On-chain behavior

Transaction flows, funding origins, related-wallet hypotheses, circular flows, concentration changes and governance activity. Wallet interaction does not by itself prove common ownership or malicious coordination.

Infrastructure and history

Domains, archived pages, repositories, releases, package metadata and public documents. A privacy service, CDN, template or young domain is context—not a verdict.

Public and market claims

Partnerships, audits, certifications, customer or volume claims, rankings, returns and promotional statements. Verify material claims at the claimed partner, auditor, regulator or primary source.

06 / Cases

How should historical failures be used?

Case studies should test whether the framework would have surfaced relevant evidence without hindsight leakage. Separate information publicly available before an event from evidence disclosed only after collapse, enforcement, litigation or investigation.

A useful case study also tests false positives: if the same warning rule is common among legitimate projects, it is a weak discriminator and should not be presented as predictive evidence.

07 / Anchors

These sources anchor methodology and regulatory context. They do not establish that any named project is compliant or non-compliant.

  • FATF — Seventh Targeted Update on Implementation of the FATF Standards on Virtual Assets/VASPs, 16 July 2026.
  • IOSCO — Policy Recommendations for Crypto and Digital Asset Markets, Final Report, 16 November 2023.
  • U.S. Treasury / OFAC — Sanctions Compliance Guidance for the Virtual Currency Industry, 15 October 2021. U.S.-specific.

FATF and IOSCO materials are international standards/recommendations, not substitutes for current national law. Regulatory status must be checked against the applicable jurisdiction's current official records.

Evidence boundary This research framework does not run blockchain surveillance, wallet clustering, fraud detection, smart-contract auditing, live alerts, identity verification or incident response. It is not financial advice, legal advice, a regulatory determination or a forensic certification. Pattern matching alone does not establish criminal intent, beneficial ownership, insolvency, market manipulation, sanctions exposure or fraud.
